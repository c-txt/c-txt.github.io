# How about Hebrews 6v4-8?

**Note:** This writeup is part of a deep dive into the doctrines of Once Saved Always Saved (OSAS) and its counterpoint which could be called Once Saved Always Striving (OSAS). "Which one is biblical?" is the question and this series examines the relevant passages one-by-one in an attempt to figure out the answer. Click here for the introductory article.

## A difficult passage

The book of Hebrews contains several severe warning passages, and chapter 6 contains one such, commonly known to be one of the most difficult to interpret. Here is the text:

> For it is impossible, in the case of those who have once been enlightened, who have tasted the heavenly gift, and have shared in the Holy Spirit, and have tasted the goodness of the word of God and the powers of the age to come, and then have fallen away, to restore them again to repentance, since they are crucifying once again the Son of God to their own harm and holding him up to contempt. For land that has drunk the rain that often falls on it, and produces a crop useful to those for whose sake it is cultivated, receives a blessing from God. But if it bears thorns and thistles, it is worthless and near to being cursed, and its end is to be burned. - Hebrews 6:4-8

This is a scary passage that some teachers use to emphasize that salvation can be lost. Is that really the case though? As always, context is king and is key to interpreting this passage.

## But first, is the writer writing to believers?

There are some who say that this passage is speaking not about saved believers but about those who are only part of a church community but not really saved. 

Earlier, I held to this view myself, until I studied the passage in greater depth and realized that no, the words used make it abundantly clear that the warning refers to those who were truly saved. For instance, the phrase "**tasted** the heavenly gift" does not refer to a mere sampling, as some hold to, because the letter to the Hebrews itself uses the same word to refer to the Lord "**tasting** death" in Heb 2:9. And then, "**shared** in the Holy Spirit" uses the greek word metachos (μέτοχος) which in other passages describes close, for example, business partnerships e.g. Luke 5:7. In Hebrews itself, we see the same word used in chapter 3:14: "For we have become **partakers** of Christ, if we hold the beginning of our confidence firm to the end." In the same way the words "heavenly gift" can only mean the gift of salvation through the Holy Spirit considering its usage elsewhere across the New Testament.   

So, I believe this passage is clearly aimed at depicting a bunch of formerly Jewish believers who had sadly fallen away from the church community. 

## The context of the passage

To understand this passage requires understanding what this repentance is referring to, and what falling away means in the context of the overall letter to the Hebrews.

The letter of Hebrews was written to Jewish  converts facing intense persecution and pressure to return to Judaism. Resultantly, some were neglecting to come to the meetings, as the author says "as is the habit of some" - Hebrews 10:25; and in this context, falling away refers to some of the converts giving in to the pressure and returning back to the rituals and practices of Judaism. 

But in falling away, they were returning to the very ones who had crucified the Lord, and thus the author says that they in a sense were crucifying the Lord all over again. In the other sense, by returning to the sacrificial system for sins, they were publicly saying that the Lord Jesus' "once for all" sacrifice wasn't enough, and that his death was merely the death of a common criminal.

This is reinforced when one understands that the word repentance can mean different things depending on the context in which it is used. For instance, at the very beginning of this chapter (Heb 6:1), the writer uses a phrase "repentance from dead works" thereby defining the context. These Hebrew believers had gone back to the dead works they had once repented of, and the writer is saying it is impossible to bring them to repentance **again**. This is clearly not referring to repentance from other sins, which, as described in other places in the bible, the believer is prone to falling into.

## But is repentance really impossible?

But before moving forward, it is also important to reconcile this passage with another key thing that the Lord said about "impossibility" in the context of the entrance to the kingdom of God.

> But he said, “The things which are impossible with men are possible with God.” - Luke 18:27

It is impossible to reconcile these two texts, unless one reads it to mean, that the writer to the Hebrews is saying that it is impossible for human preachers or teachers to restore these believers. That this is the correct reading is manifest, because in just the previous verse he talks about not laying again the foundation of repentance from dead works saying however that "This will we do, **if** God permits." 

## More on the falling away

Another passage in Hebrews makes it abundantly clear the context of falling away.

> Beware, brothers, lest perhaps there might be in any one of you an evil heart of unbelief, in falling away from the living God; - Hebrews 3:12 

In this verse too, clearly the author is writing to believers for he calls them brothers. And he mentions the possibility of unbelief and falling away, though the greek verb used here for falling away (ἀφίστημι) is different from that used in Heb 6 (παραπίπτω) which is an even more stronger word for falling away, implying a wilful rejection of Christ. 

That aside, what really is the unbelief and falling away is he referring to? These questions are critical to answer to understand the entire letter to the Hebrews and to clear up many doubts about the potential loss of salvation. 

In Hebrews 3, the author refers to a rebellion, comparing it to be similar to the Hebrews falling away. This was the rebellion of the entire nation of Israel, when the twelve spies returned from surveying the promised land. That's when God swore in His wrath "They will not enter into my rest" Heb 3:11

The question to ask here is, if salvation is metaphorically represented by Israel being redeemed from Egypt, did the Israelites ever return to Egypt? Rather they perished in the desert. The punishment they experienced was not entering the promised land, the land of blessing, the land of milk and honey. They were afraid of the giants in the land, even as the Hebrew believers were afraid of the giants of pressure and persecution. But they never returned to the land of slavery. They were saved once for all. 

## Entering the promised land of blessing

The passage we are considering, ends with two types of land. The land which is blessed and land which is **close** to being cursed. Note that it does not say land that is cursed. 

A saved believer, once saved is always saved. But if he turns back in fear from the consequences of moving forward to occupy the promised blessings, such as persecution, he or she is near to being cursed. The state is almost similar to that of an unbeliever, and is sure to result in harsh judgement from the Lord, the judgement of fire similar to what Paul warned the erring Corinthian believer of.

On the other hand, the believer who keeps moving forward, fighting against the giants of disbelief, fear, self, and sin, go on to enter God's promised blessing of rest. This is a teaching on judgement and rewards, as expanded elsewhere in this letter:

> For we know him who said, “Vengeance belongs to me. I will repay,” says the Lord. Again, “The Lord will **judge his people**.”  Heb 10:30

> to God the **Judge of all**, to the spirits of just men - Heb 12:23

> Let marriage be held in honor among all, and let the bed be undefiled; but God will **judge the sexually immoral and adulterers**. - Heb 13: 4

> Therefore don’t throw away your boldness, which has a great **reward**. - Heb 10: 35

> By faith Moses, when he had grown up, refused to be called the son of Pharaoh’s daughter, choosing rather to share ill treatment with God’s people than to enjoy the pleasures of sin for a time, considering the reproach of Christ greater riches than the treasures of Egypt; for he looked to the **reward**. - Heb 11:24-26

## Tying up the loose ends

The letter to the Hebrews is a very dense letter, written primarily to a Jewish audience and hence its context must be rightly understood to ensure the intent of its author is not misunderstood. May this be our journey, as we not only read but study its warning passages, so that we can take its message to heart.

It took me many months of study, as I went through books, articles, and videos to finally resolve the perplexities about this passage that had been introduced to me by opposing views. However, having studied these issues for myself, in depth, I am now convinced more than ever that salvation once gained cannot be lost.
