# What about Romans 8:13?

>  For if you live after the flesh, you must die; but if by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the body, you will live. - Romans 8:13 (WEB)

Here we face a troubling question. Paul clearly says "if you live after the flesh, you must die." What does this death mean? Clearly, from the context, this verse is writen to believers. Teachers holding to the doctrine of Once Saved Always Striving (OSAS), often use this verse to argue that believers living after the flesh, or in other words, living a carnal life, will lose their salvation. They often compare it to the Genesis account of God saying that Adam would die if he ate of the fruit. And clearly Adam died both spiritually and physically.

But are the proponents of the Once Saved Always Striving (OSAS) doctrine right in using this verse in this way? Does this verse actually teach that those Christians who live after the flesh die spiritually **and** lose their salvation? Or rather has the verse been misused, twisted deliberately or in ignorance, and taken out of context? This article is my attempt to answer these questions for myself after a long hard struggle, and perhaps may also help others who may be confused by similar arguments.

## Physical or spiritual death?

It is always helpful to examine the context to derive the actual meaning of the verse. But before even getting into the context, in this case the meaning of the verse becomes abundantly clear merely by looking at the greek word  apothnēskō (ἀποθνήσκω) used in this verse. This is a verb that means, as defined by the Liddell, Scott, Jones Ancient Greek Lexicon (LSJ), to die or to be put to death by judicial sentencing. 

The important thing to note is that in all the 122 times this verb is used in the New Testament (WEB version), it always refers to physical death or being put to physical death. You can check out the references section at the bottom of this page for a full list of all the verses this verb has been used and go through them to confirm. Three of these one hundred and twenty two verses (which are marked with a star in the references list) are quite interesting and debatable in their use of the word die. But unquestionably in the overwhelming majority of cases, if not all, this word refers to physical death, or in other words the first death. 

Note also, Jesus' promise of eternal life in John 3:16 does not use this verb when he says "whoever believes in him should not **perish** but have eternal life," rather he uses the greek verb apolluō (ἀπολλύω), meaning "destroy", which is of course much stronger than the verb "kill," and clearly refers to the second death.

## Death as a judicial punishment

In fact, the verse in question actually prefixes another verb  μελλετε (mellō) which means to be about to, or to be on the point of. So the verse which is often translated as "you must die," is actually better translated as "you are about to die," or "you are going to die," as is seen translated in hyper-literal translations. This only indicates the great grace and patience of God as He gives unrepentant believers numerous opportunities to turn from their fleshly ways before handing them over to physical death.

This idea of judicial punishment is borne out by the advice Paul gave to the Corinthian church to deal with the scandalous incident that occured in their church. 

> you are to deliver such a one to Satan for the destruction of the flesh, that the spirit may be saved in the day of the Lord Jesus. - 1 Cor 5:5

However, even in this case, Paul later in his second epistle recommends forgiving the one who had been cast out of the church fellowship, presumably now because he had sorrowed and repented of the sin.

> This punishment which was inflicted by the many is sufficient for such a one; so that, on the contrary, you should rather forgive him and comfort him, lest by any means such a one should be swallowed up with his excessive sorrow. Therefore I beg you to confirm your love toward him. - 2 Cor 2:6-7

In a nutshell, this case goes to show that even in the case of Christians living terribly carnal lifestyles, they are not beyond the grace and forgiveness of God even if they die in their sin. However, God does desire all believers to repent rather than to put them to death because of unrepentant and flagrantly sinful lifestyles.

## Concrete can reinforce, so can context 

Even the larger context of the Romans 8:13 verse, makes it very clear that Paul is not talking about loss of salvation. Let's read from the beginning of chapter 8 for the context:

 There is therefore now no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus, who don’t walk according to the flesh, but according to the Spirit. - Romans 8:1

But wait. There is a "therefore" here which leads us to look back even further to Chapter 7 for the context. So what does the previous few verses say? The answer is kind of shocking. The great apostle Paul acknowledges and confesses that he sees even in himself a great struggle with sin.

>  For we know that the law is spiritual, but I am fleshly, sold under sin. For I don’t understand what I am doing. For I don’t practice what I desire to do; but what I hate, that I do.  But if what I don’t desire, that I do, I consent to the law that it is good. So now it is no more I that do it, but sin which dwells in me. For I know that in me, that is, in my flesh, dwells no good thing. For desire is present with me, but I don’t find it doing that which is good. For the good which I desire, I don’t do; but the evil which I don’t desire, that I practice. But if what I don’t desire, that I do, it is no more I that do it, but sin which dwells in me. I find then the law that, while I desire to do good, evil is present. For I delight in God’s law after the inward person, but I see a different law in my members, warring against the law of my mind, and bringing me into captivity under the law of sin which is in my members. What a wretched man I am! Who will deliver me out of the body of this death? - Romans 7:14-24

Paul was absolutely not a hypocrite about his struggles. With stark transparency, he says that at times he "practices evil." Note also that as he writes his great letter of theology to the Romans, he does not write this confession in the past tense but clearly in the present tense.  What a confession! Note also the distinction he makes between the law in his "inward person" or "mind" and law in "his members" or "body" and how he depicts the war between them which causes his struggles.

And then, He continues to say how he serves both good and evil, one with the mind and the other with the flesh: 

> "What a wretched man I am! Who will deliver me out of the body of this death? 25 I thank God through Jesus Christ, our Lord! So then with the mind, I myself serve God’s law, but with the flesh, sin’s law." - Romans 7:24b-25

## The law of the Spirit of life

As we have seen, Paul's confession of sin in Chapter 7 provides the context for the next chapter 8 which contains the verse in question. And at the very beginning of this chapter we find this great statement:

> There is therefore now no condemnation to those who are in Christ Jesus, who don’t walk according to the flesh, but according to the Spirit. - Romans 8:1

As we read further in Chapter 8 we come across the next few verses:

> For the law of the Spirit of life in Christ Jesus made me free from the law of sin and of death. For what the law couldn’t do, in that it was weak through the flesh, God did, sending his own Son in the likeness of sinful flesh and for sin, he condemned sin in the flesh, that the ordinance of the law might be fulfilled in **us** who don’t walk **according** to the flesh, but **according** to the Spirit. For those who live according to the flesh set their minds on the things of the flesh, but those who live according to the Spirit, the things of the Spirit. " - Romans 8:2-5

Lo and behold, Paul now uses the word "us" to include himself in the category of folk who are not walking according to the flesh. Yet, in just the previous chapter he had written that he does **struggle** with sin. Clearly, he means he is not habitually walking according to the flesh though at times he does struggle. Also, keep a note of his use of the word "according" which we will discuss next.

As we read further in this passage we see not only the distinction between carnal Christians and spiritual Christians, but another distinction as well. The distinction between saved and unsaved. This is made clearer by Paul's change in the use of his words. No longer does he use "according to the flesh" but "in the flesh." And this is a major change:

> Those who are **in the flesh** can’t please God. But **you** are not in the flesh but **in the Spirit**, if it is so that the Spirit of God dwells in you. But if any man doesn’t have the Spirit of Christ, he is not his. If Christ is in you, the body is dead because of sin, but the spirit is alive because of righteousness. But if the Spirit of him who raised up Jesus from the dead dwells in you, he who raised up Christ Jesus from the dead will also give life to your mortal bodies through his Spirit who dwells in you. - Romans 8: 8-11

Here is the clear difference. If anyone is saved, is born again, he/she has the indwelling Spirit of God. If anyone does not have the indwelling Spirit, he/she is in the flesh and not in the Spirit and so can not please God. But the one who is in the Spirit only has life to look forward to even after the mortal body dies.

## Summing Up

It is easy to get lost and muddled when teachers teach an incorrect gospel based on their lack of understanding of the overwhelming magnificience of God's offer of a free salvation, without any strings attached. But arriving at the truth does not need a deep knowledge of grammar or greek, just a humble listening for the voice and teaching of the Spirit. 

The overwhelming message of the Bible is that of free and full grace. Take Adam. He ate of the fruit and died. But he was among the first to hear from God of the promise of the Messiah. One can only wonder that perhaps he too in faith accepted the eternal gospel and will be in Heaven with us one day. And though he sinned, and though he died, his death was not without the hope of grace.
