# What About Romans 11v22?

## Introduction

The debate on Eternal Security of the Believer, a.k.a Once Saved Always Saved (OSAS), and on the other hand the Eternal Insecurity of the Believer, which we could term Once Saved Forever Striving (OSFS), never really bothered me until recently. 

I have confidently stood on the OSAS side for long and was never in doubt about it, primarily due to my own experience of repeatedly falling and being made to rise again through the incessant work of the Holy Spirit. 

This is despite the fact that I have, for several years now, abandoned my Calvinistic thinking which had been my default for many years due to the Christian tradition in which I had been brought up. But while I am not a Calvinist today, on the other hand I have been unable to completely swallow the Arminian counterpoints, especially the last bit that says that salvation once gained can be lost. Could the truth lie somewhere in between?

Recently, spurred by some debates among Christian friends, I decided to take a relook at this once again and to research and write about it, both to clarify my thinking and to settle the matter in my mind once and for all.  

And even as I do this seeking to strengthen my own faith and beliefs, I hope that this series of articles may help someone else too at some point as they examine the relevant verses one by one, just as I have gained much through reading others' writings (on both sides) on this topic. 

One thing I must mention is that I found when I explored this topic, that Arminus himself wasn't sure on the last point of Arminianism, namely, if a believer could lose salvation. 

## So, what about Romans 11:22?

> See then the goodness and severity of God. Toward those who fell, severity; but toward you, goodness, *if you continue in his goodness; otherwise you also will be cut off.* - Romans 11:22 (NASB)

It does sound scary because the letter to the Romans is addressed to believers. As Paul addreses his audience in chapter 1, he writes 

>"among whom you are also called to belong to Jesus Christ; to all who are in Rome, beloved of God, called to be saints: Grace to you and peace from God our Father and the Lord Jesus Christ." - Romans 1:6,7

So, if that were the only factor, that this letter is written to believers, then seen in isolation chapter 11:22 does seem to imply that a believer could be cut off from the olive tree and lose salvation in case he does not continue in God's goodness. But one must remember, that Romans is a dense theological treatise, and Paul switches audiences in different sections of the letter. Take for example, Chapter 2 verse 1 which addresses the moralist, and then continues to speak specifically to him over the next many verses:

> Therefore you are without excuse, O man, whoever you are who judge. - Romans 2:1 (WEB)  

More pertinently, in this case Paul in Chapter 11 is addressing the gentiles, and more specifically the gentiles as a class. See Chapter 11: 13a which clearly says:

> For I speak to you who are Gentiles. - Romans 11:13a (WEB)

Paul has just finished in the earlier part of chapter 11 speaking about the Israelites as a class being hardened. And now he turns to the gentiles who have obtained salvation through the fall of the Israelites and warns them against being proud. It is in this context that he says, that the gentiles too can be cut off.

But the clincher for me is this word "otherwise." Most translations translate the original as "if you continue in His kindness; for otherwise you too will be cut off." This is sometimes used to show that "see, salvation is conditional!". However, I noticed that only in Darby's translation (and probably because of his strong dispensationalist views) he has translated this verse as:

> if thou shalt abide in goodness, since [otherwise] *thou* also wilt be cut away.

As per Darby's translation notes: Square brackets in the text indicate (a) words added to complete the sense in English similar to those shown in italics in the Authorised Version; or (b), words as to which there are variations in the original manuscripts. 

Darby had his own interpretative bias just like all translations have their own interpretative bias, however in this case because of his more literal translation, he was frank enough to indicate that the word otherwise is but an alternative translation of the word inserted for potential clarity. 

## But should the word be translated as "otherwise"?

Ἴδε οὖν ⸂χρηστότητα καὶ ἀποτομίαν θεοῦ· ἐπὶ μὲν τοὺς πεσόντας, ἀποτομίαν· ἐπὶ δέ σε, χρηστότητα,⸃ ἐὰν ἐπιμείνῃς τῇ χρηστότητι· *ἐπεὶ* καὶ σὺ ἐκκοπήσῃ.

Most of the translations translate ἐπεὶ as otherwise, since perhaps otherwise it doesn't make sense to their theological views. However the primary meaning of the word is "Since." See for example other places where this word has been used. It occurs around 26 times and is translated in the ESV as "because" in Matt 18:32; Matt 21:46; "since" in Matt 27:6; Mark 15:42; Luke 1:34; John 13:29; John 19:31; 1 Cor 5:10; 1 Cor 14:12; 2 Cor 11:18; 2 Cor 13:3; Heb 2:14; Heb 3:6; Heb 5:2; Heb 5:11; Heb 6:13; Heb 9:17 "then" in Romans 3:6; Heb 9:26; Heb 11:11; and "otherwise" only in Romans 11:6; Romans 11:22;  1 Cor 7:14; 1 Cor 14:16; 1 Cor 15:29; Heb 10:2.

My point of quoting all these verses is only to show that the use of the word "otherwise" in most translation is merely a translator's choice. It could have been translated "since" as Darby's literal translation does and as it is done in the many other places in most translations as quoted above. Because, if Paul's intent when writing this was "since" rather than "otherwise", it completely changes the meaning.

> if thou shalt abide in goodness, *since thou also wilt be cut away.*

If the word "since" is Paul's original intent, it logically just introduces what he will say in the following verses about the "fullness of the gentiles":

> a partial hardening has happened to Israel, until the fullness of the Gentiles has come in - from Romans 11:25

To sum, it seems that Paul is saying in v22, not a conditional cutting off of the gentiles as a class, but rather a definite cutting off. Just like the Israelites as a class, as mentioned in the beginning of the chapter, have been cut off for now, once the last gentile has come in and the rest of the gentiles as a class harden their hearts, they too as a class **will** be cut off. 

So, in effect what he might be saying is, "gentiles as a class, you should not boast over the Israelites who have been cut off, since ultimately you too will be cut off". So the whole verse could be read to say that gentiles as a class will continue to experience God's goodness as long as they continue to respond to God's goodness, but then after that they will be cut off.

In the end, whether the last bit on the potential mistranslation of the word ἐπεὶ is taken into account or not, the overall context of the passage is an address to the gentiles as a class of people, not to individual believers. So, with all my limited understanding, I do not believe that this verse supports the OSFS view in any way.
